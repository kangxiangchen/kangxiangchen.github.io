[Auto]Stitching Photo Mosaics

Chen KangxiangCS180, Project 4 2026-10-28

When a camera only rotates, every photo it takes sees the same rays of light, just cut by a differently tilted image plane. Any two such photos are therefore related by a homography, a 3×3 projective transform, whatever the depth of the scene. In Part A I recover those homographies from points I clicked by hand, warp photos onto a common plane with my own inverse-mapping code, and blend them into mosaics. In Part B the clicking is replaced by Harris corners, descriptor matching and RANSAC, following Brown, Szeliski and Winder (2005).

Conventions used throughout: a point is (x, y) = (column, row) with pixel centres at integer coordinates, and computeH(p1, p2) returns the H with p2 ~ H p1, i.e. it maps image-1 coordinates into image 2. Photos are downsized so the longer side is 1800 px before any processing.

Part A: Image warping and mosaicing

A.1 Shooting the pictures

I stood in one spot and rotated the phone between shots, with roughly half of each photo overlapping the next. Rotating about the centre of projection is what makes the relation between photos a pure homography regardless of scene depth. Moving the camera instead introduces parallax, which no single homography can undo.

A.2 Recovering homographies

A homography maps p = (x, y, 1) to p' ~ Hp. Fixing the bottom-right entry to 1 leaves 8 unknowns:

x' = (a x + b y + c) / (g x + h y + 1)
y' = (d x + e y + f) / (g x + h y + 1)

Multiplying out the denominator makes each equation linear in the unknowns, so every correspondence gives two rows of a linear system:

[ x  y  1  0  0  0  -x x'  -y x' ] h = x'
[ 0  0  0  x  y  1  -x y'  -y y' ] h = y'

Stacking n correspondences gives a 2n × 8 system Ah = b. Four points determine H exactly, but then every pixel of clicking error goes straight into H, so I click more points and solve the overdetermined system by least squares, minimising ||Ah − b||². Before solving, both point sets are normalised (centroid to the origin, mean distance √2) and H is un-normalised afterwards. In exact arithmetic this gives the same answer, but it keeps A well conditioned: raw pixel coordinates put entries of size 1 next to entries of size 106 (the x·x' terms).

A.3 Warping and rectification

Warping uses inverse mapping. I push the four corners of the input through H to get the output bounding box, then for every output pixel apply H−1 to its location to find where it came from in the input, and read the input there. Looping over output pixels guarantees every one gets a value, so there are no holes. An output pixel whose source lands outside the input gets alpha 0, and that alpha mask is reused for blending.

Nearest neighbour rounds the source location to the closest pixel centre. Bilinear takes the four surrounding pixels and weights them by (1−fx)(1−fy), fx(1−fy), (1−fx)fy and fxfy, where fx and fy are the fractional offsets. Both are written from scratch and vectorised with NumPy.

To test the warp I rectified photos of rectangular objects: click the object's four corners and map them to a rectangle with the object's true aspect ratio. Only a margin around the object is shown, because points near the vanishing line map towards infinity.

Averaged over my rectifications, nearest neighbour took 0.07 s and bilinear 0.21 s, about 2.8× slower: bilinear reads four pixels and does several multiply-adds per output pixel where nearest neighbour reads one. In the zoomed crops, nearest neighbour shows jagged, stair-stepped edges, because neighbouring output pixels jump between whole input pixels, and where the warp stretches the image each source pixel is copied into a block. Bilinear gives smooth edges and gradients at the cost of slight blurring. For a final mosaic the extra time is negligible next to the quality gain, so I use bilinear everywhere else.

A.4 Blending into a mosaic

For each set I keep the middle photo unwarped as the reference and warp the others into its image plane. Homographies are computed between neighbouring photos and chained, for example H0→2 = H1→2H0→1, and a photo to the right of the reference uses the inverse of its pairwise H. The canvas is the union of all warped bounding boxes, and every photo is warped into it in one pass.

Each photo gets a weight map equal to its distance to its own nearest border (1 at the centre, falling linearly to 0 at the edges), warped along with the colours. Pasting photos on top of each other (overwrite) leaves hard seams. Plain feathering, the weighted average ΣwiIi / Σwi, hides the seams but ghosts any fine detail that is misaligned by a pixel or more. My final results use two-band blending (Brown and Lowe): each photo is split into a low band (Gaussian blur, σ = 2.0 px, computed only from valid pixels so the empty canvas doesn't bleed in) and a high band (photo minus low band). The low band is feathered, so exposure differences fade smoothly. The high band at each pixel comes from the single photo with the largest weight, so fine detail is never averaged and cannot ghost.

A.5 Bells and whistles: cylindrical projection

A planar mosaic stretches badly towards the sides as the field of view grows, since the plane is tangent at the reference view and the stretch grows like tan θ. Instead I project onto a cylinder of radius f around the camera, and sample directly from the original photos so nothing is resampled twice. For output pixel (u, v), θ = u/f and height h = v/f give the ray (sin θ, h, cos θ). That ray hits the reference image plane at (f tan θ + cx, f h / cos θ + cy), which Hi−1 maps into photo i, where I sample bilinearly. Blending is the same two-band scheme. I set f = 0.95 × image width, which matches a phone's main camera held upright. A pure pan becomes a pure horizontal shift on the cylinder, so vertical lines stay vertical and the sides are no longer stretched.

Part B: Automatic stitching

B.1 Harris corners and ANMS

Harris corners use the corner strength det(M)/trace(M) of the second moment matrix M (Gaussian σ = 1), keeping 3×3 local maxima and discarding corners within 20 px of the border so a full descriptor window fits. This returns tens of thousands of corners, crowded onto high-contrast texture. Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS) gives each corner the radius ri = minj ||xi − xj|| over corners j with f(xi) < 0.9 f(xj), the distance to the nearest clearly stronger corner, and keeps the 500 with the largest radius. A weak corner survives only if nothing much stronger is nearby, so the kept points are strong and spread evenly, which matters because a homography fitted to points in one corner of the overlap extrapolates poorly.

B.2 Feature descriptors

Around each corner I take a 40×40 window, blur it (Gaussian σ = 2) and sample an 8×8 grid with 5 px spacing, centred exactly on the corner. Sampling sparsely from a blurred large window makes the descriptor insensitive to the small position errors of the corner detector. Each descriptor is then bias/gain normalised to zero mean and unit standard deviation, so brightness or contrast differences between photos do not matter.

B.3 Feature matching

For each descriptor in photo 1, I find the nearest and second-nearest descriptors in photo 2 by squared error, and accept the match if e1-NN/e2-NN < 0.5 (Lowe's ratio test). A distinctive correct match is far better than its runner-up, while on repetitive texture the best and second-best candidates are about equally good, so their ratio is close to 1. MOPS Figure 6b shows correct matches concentrated at low ratios and incorrect ones near 1, and the threshold sits in the gap between them. The histogram shows the ratio for every feature of one of my photos, with the threshold marked.

B.4 RANSAC and automatic mosaics

The ratio test still lets some wrong matches through, and least squares is ruined by even one bad outlier. 4-point RANSAC repeats 5000 times: pick 4 random matches, compute the exact H through them, and count the inliers whose reprojection error ||Hp − p'|| is under 3.0 px. It keeps the largest inlier set and refits H by least squares on all of its points. If a fraction w of matches is correct, a random sample is all-inlier with probability w4. Even with w = 0.3, the chance of never drawing a clean sample in 5000 tries is about 10−18.

B.5 Bells and whistles: rotation-invariant descriptors

Each corner gets an orientation: the direction of the image gradient, averaged with a Gaussian window (σ = 4.5) as in MOPS. The 8×8 sampling grid is rotated by that angle before sampling, so the descriptor is expressed in the corner's own frame, and the same corner seen in a rotated photo yields the same descriptor. To score it against ground truth, I rotated one of my photos by 30 degrees with my own warp, so the true H is known, and matched it against the original:

  • Axis-aligned descriptors: 8 inliers out of 12 matches, max error 778.07 px.
  • Rotation-invariant descriptors: 276 inliers out of 300 matches, max error 0.07 px.

The error is the largest distance between where the recovered and the true H send a grid of points over the photo.
